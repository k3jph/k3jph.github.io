In [1973](https://sabr.org/journal/article/the-historical-evolution-of-the-designated-hitter-rule/),
the American League decided that one of its nine players did not have
to play baseball. The pitcher would hit, they ruled, only until he
wouldn't. The [designated hitter rule](https://www.mlb.com/glossary/rules/designated-hitter-rule)
was sold as modernization. It was actually an admission of failure,
institutionalized and made permanent.

The failure was this: MLB had spent decades developing pitchers who
couldn't hit, and rather than fix that, the league built a rule to
accommodate it. The DH did not solve a problem. It preserved one.

## The Self-Fulfilling Prophecy

The argument for the DH has always rested on the same premise: pitchers
can't hit, so why make them try. What the argument never acknowledges
is that pitchers can't hit because nobody made them try. Once the
American League removed the requirement, it removed the development
pathway. Minor league pitchers stopped taking batting practice seriously.
Coaches stopped evaluating them at the plate. Front offices stopped
caring. Fifty years later, the same people point at the results and
say: see, pitchers can't hit.

This is not an iron law of nature. It is a policy outcome dressed up
as a natural fact.

The National League understood this, at least implicitly, which is why
it resisted the designated hitter for nearly fifty years. The holdout
ended in 2022, the universal DH arriving via collective bargaining as
a permanent feature of a game that had briefly adopted it during the
COVID-shortened 2020 season.[^pandemic] The last normal full season of pitchers hitting in the National League
was 2019. Make a note of it.

## What the DH Costs

The designated hitter does not merely replace an automatic out with
a better hitter. It removes an entire decision layer from the game.

When the pitcher bats, the manager has to think. In the sixth inning,
with runners on and the pitcher due up third, do you let him hit and
keep him in the game, or do you pinch hit and go to the bullpen? That
decision cascades: who comes in, when, in what order. The [double switch](https://www.baseball-reference.com/bullpen/Double_switch)
exists because of the pitcher's spot in the lineup. Pull the pitcher's
spot and you pull the geometry of late-game strategy with it.

The DH game is not a faster, more exciting version of baseball. It
is a shallower one. More offense, less chess. The tradeoff is real and
the people who made it chose wrong.

## Shohei Ohtani

Ohtani came up through [Nippon Professional Baseball](https://npb.jp/eng/),
but not through a no-DH environment.[^npb] He played for the
[Hokkaido Nippon-Ham Fighters](https://www.fighters.co.jp/en/) in the
Pacific League, which adopted the designated hitter in 1975. That
matters. Ohtani was not simply produced by a rule that forced pitchers
to bat. He was produced by an organization willing to let an
extraordinary player remain whole.

By the time Ohtani [arrived with the Angels in 2018](https://www.baseball-reference.com/players/o/ohtansh01.shtml),
he was already both things: a hitter with legitimate power and a
starting pitcher with elite strikeout ability. He did not become a
two-way player in MLB. He arrived as one.

The response from American baseball commentators was, predictably, that
he was exceptional. One in a million. A once-in-a-generation talent.
All of that may be true at his level of performance. But the argument
proves too much. What it actually shows is that MLB's development
infrastructure, shaped around the DH assumption for half a century in
the American League and now universally, had largely stopped producing
serious two-way major leaguers. Ohtani looks miraculous because the
context that would have made him merely excellent was systematically
dismantled.

Ohtani is not proof the Japanese make everything better, faster, and
cheaper. He is simply the proof that the rule was wrong.[^japan]

## The Deeper Problem

Baseball sold the DH as progress. More scoring, more excitement, fewer
automatic outs. This is the same logic that would improve chess by
removing pawns. Technically, the pieces that remain are more powerful.
The game that results is not better. It is smaller.

The pitcher's spot in the lineup was not dead weight. It was constraint,
and constraint is where strategy lives. Remove the weak point and you
remove the decisions that the weak point forces. The manager of a DH
lineup has one fewer lever to pull and one fewer thing to think about.
Over the course of a season, across thousands of at-bats and hundreds
of late-game decisions, that adds up to a meaningfully less interesting
sport.

The universal DH is here. It is not going away. But that does not mean
it was right, and it does not mean that Ohtani's career should be read
as a vindication of the modern game. It should be read as a rebuke of it.

He showed up to play baseball. All of it. The league spent fifty years
building a rule so its players wouldn't have to.

[^pandemic]: The universal DH was first adopted in the abbreviated 2020 season and then became permanent through the 2022 collective bargaining agreement between MLB and the Players Association. The owners pushed for it; the players accepted it as part of a broader deal. Neither side was thinking about what it cost the game.

[^npb]: The Pacific League adopted the designated hitter in 1975, two years after the American League. The Central League resisted it for decades and has only recently voted to adopt it starting in 2027. Ohtani developed as a two-way player inside a DH league, which makes the Fighters' willingness to keep him whole more significant, not less.

[^japan]: Yes, this invokes a fifty-year-old stereotype about Japanese manufacturing culture that died roughly thirty-five years ago. Shohei Ohtani is thirty-one. Do the math.
