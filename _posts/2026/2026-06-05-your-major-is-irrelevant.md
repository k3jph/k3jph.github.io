I was watching a video recently in which someone complained about having
a bachelor's degree and being unable to find work. A stitch appeared
almost immediately with the retort that was already loading in every
comment section: you did not even tell us what your major was. Both
are familiar refrains. Both go viral on a regular basis. And as I was
making myself an espresso the other morning, something clicked: they
are both right. I have an area studies degree and I am making espresso.

Except that is not quite the confession it sounds like.

I finished my mathematics degree in 2002. My public policy doctorate
in 2014. I had been working in data science, machine learning, and
artificial intelligence for years before I ever enrolled in an East
Asian Studies program. I completed that degree in 2020, as a working
professional with a full career behind me, because I wanted to study
something I found genuinely interesting. Not for a credential. Not for
a pivot. Because I wanted to know things. The espresso is just coffee.

What I want to say to both parties in that video is this: you agreed
on the wrong premise before either of you opened your mouths.

## The Premise Neither Side Questioned

The person who cannot find work with a bachelor's degree believes that
college is job training and that the training was defective. The person
demanding to know the major believes that college is job training and
that the first person chose the wrong trade. They are having a genuine
disagreement about which curriculum produces better tradespeople.
Neither one is questioning whether the trade school model is the right
way to think about a university education.

It is not.

Outside fields where a specific degree is a prerequisite for
professional licensure, employers doing cognitively demanding work
usually stop caring very much about your major after the first few
years. What they care about is whether you can
read carefully, reason under pressure, construct an argument from
evidence, and learn what you do not know when a problem demands it.
These are not job skills. They are the qualities of a better person.
That is what a serious education produces. That it also tends to make
you more employable is real, but it is a byproduct, not the point.

## What an Area Studies Degree Produced

My capstone project in East Asian Studies applied Eastern philosophical
frameworks to the ethics of autonomous vehicle decision-making. The
problem is real and hard: self-driving systems will encounter
situations in which a crash is unavoidable and choices must be made,
in milliseconds, about how harm is distributed. Most of the people
working on that problem are engineers. Most of the philosophical
resources being brought to bear come from a single tradition in Western
The dominant frameworks applied to these decisions draw on
[trolley-problem scenarios and utilitarian tradeoffs](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0637-6).
The question I was asking was
what Confucian frameworks of relational obligation, or Daoist
conceptions of contextual harmony and naturalness, contribute to
that conversation that the utilitarian account leaves out.

This is not an exercise in cultural flavor. It is a substantive
philosophical intervention into one of the most consequential design
questions in contemporary AI. It requires exactly the kind of
cross-cultural understanding that an area studies program develops.
An engineering degree does not teach you to ask it. A philosophy
degree from a department with a Western canon orientation is unlikely
to equip you to answer it. The area studies degree produced the work.

I am still waiting to hear which majors are useless.

## The Right-Wing Attack and What It Costs

For years, the political right has run a
[sustained campaign](https://www.usnews.com/news/education-news/articles/2026-01-02/the-biggest-developments-in-higher-education-policy-in-2025)
against universities. The indictment is familiar: overpriced,
ideologically captured, producing unemployable graduates with grievances
instead of skills. There is a legitimate concern buried in there about
the cost of higher education and the gap between credential inflation
and genuine learning. That concern has been largely abandoned in favor
of something less honest. Between 2015 and 2024,
[Republican confidence in higher education fell from 56 percent to 20 percent](https://news.gallup.com/poll/646880/confidence-higher-education-closely-divided.aspx),
according to Gallup. That is not a response to declining quality. It
is the result of a decade of deliberate delegitimization.

The conclusion that follows, in policy terms, is that universities
should be defunded, discredited, or replaced with vocational training.
This is self-defeating in a way that almost never gets named directly.
You cannot simultaneously want a competitive, knowledge-driven economy
and systematically delegitimize the institutions that produce
knowledge. You cannot spend a decade telling people that expertise is
a form of arrogance and then be confused when the supply of expertise
declines.

What has happened, in practice, is that in much of our political
rhetoric, ["elite" has been quietly redefined](https://www.niskanencenter.org/how-the-diploma-divide-transformed-american-politics/).
It no longer describes the powerful or the wealthy. It describes the
educated. The banker is respectable. The professor is
suspect. A billionaire who dropped out of college is a folk hero. A
high school teacher with a master's degree is part of the problem.
This is not an analysis of higher education. It is a cultural
resentment with a policy costume on.

The person in the stitch video is not a political operative. But the
"what was your major" retort lands differently after years of
preparation. When we have spent a generation teaching people that
humanities and social science degrees are punchlines, the punchline
becomes reflexive. The sneerers did not write the joke. They inherited
it.

## What Education Actually Is

The defense of liberal education keeps being made on the wrong terms.
It gets framed as a jobs argument: workforce development, transferable
skills, economic mobility, STEM investment. These are real goods and
they are not nothing. But they concede the central point before the
argument begins. If the only thing you can say for a literature degree
is that it makes you a more versatile hire, you have already agreed
that the purpose of a degree is employment, and you are arguing about
efficiency on terms the other side set.

The stronger claim, the one that is almost never made in polite policy
discourse, is that education is not job training. It makes you a better
person. That is the whole argument. Everything else follows from it.
[Last year I wrote](/2025/08/07/the-ancient-dignity-of-education)
about the 1595 imperial diploma elevating Thomas Arundell to the
nobility. The document justified his status first by noting that he
had devoted himself to the liberal disciplines, traveled, encountered
other cultures, and formed his judgment. Not a battle. Not an estate.
The cultivation of the mind as a form of honor is not a historical
curiosity. It is the view that held for most of Western intellectual
history, and it is the view we have spent the last several decades
carefully dismantling.

[As I argued recently](/2026/05/22/economics-is-not-the-study-of-money/),
disciplines do not map neatly onto functions, and pretending they do
produces a badly distorted picture of how intellectual work actually
proceeds. My policy training does not flow directly into machine
learning. It shapes how I think about what machine learning is for,
who it affects, and how its consequences should be weighed. Those
questions are not solved by engineering. They require people who have
been trained to think about context, history, and the distribution
of consequences across populations. That is not merely a STEM competency.
It is an education competency.

## What I Tell My Students

At some point every semester, a student asks me what they should major
in if they want a good job. I give them the same answer every time:
I do not know, nobody does, and that is not the question worth
spending much energy on.

The question worth asking is what you find genuinely interesting. What
problems make you want to understand them better? What would you still
want to study if there were no credential waiting at the end? Start
there. The discipline will teach you to think in ways you cannot
fully anticipate in advance, which is part of what makes it work.
College will not tell you what job to do. It will make you a better
person. That is worth more than any credential, and it is the one
thing that compounds over a lifetime.

I have a mathematics degree, an East Asian Studies degree, a doctorate
in public policy, and several graduate degrees in fields that do not
obviously connect to each other or to my current work. None of them
maps cleanly onto a job title. All of them shaped how I approach a
problem. That is the point. It has always been the point.

The espresso was excellent, by the way.
