I have built a virtual museum exhibition. This was not the plan.

[*Judith and Holofernes: The Fatal Image*](https://judith.jameshoward.us)
is now open at
[judith.jameshoward.us](https://judith.jameshoward.us). It brings
together [58 works](https://judith.jameshoward.us/gallery) depicting
Judith, Holofernes, and the remarkably unstable meanings artists have
found in their story.

This began at the [Walters Art
Museum](https://art.thewalters.org/object/37.653/) in Baltimore with
[Trophime Bigot's extraordinary candlelit
painting](https://judith.jameshoward.us/artworks/bigot-walters-judith)
of Judith beheading Holofernes. The scene emerges from darkness around
a single flame. Judith works with grim concentration while her maid
holds the light. Holofernes is still alive, but not for long.

I did not know then that I was looking at one image in a conversation
that had already been going on for centuries.

The [Book of Judith](https://judith.jameshoward.us/story) tells the
story of a Jewish city facing destruction by an invading army. Its
leaders have nearly exhausted their courage when Judith, a wealthy and
devout widow, decides to act. She enters the enemy camp, allows
Holofernes to believe he understands her intentions, waits until he is
drunk, and kills him with his own sword. She returns to her people
carrying his head. The invading army, deprived of its commander and
apparently also its capacity for independent thought, collapses.

The story is short, brutal, and almost infinitely reusable.

Judith becomes a pious widow, civic heroine, tyrant-slayer, political
allegory, model of chastity, seductress, dangerous woman, national
symbol, femme fatale, feminist icon, or nightmare. Sometimes the artist
shows the killing. Sometimes Judith appears after the sword has fallen,
serenely displaying the head. Sometimes her maid is barely present.
Sometimes the two women operate as a highly efficient team. Sometimes
Holofernes dominates the scene even while dying. In other works, he has
been reduced to the object Judith carries away.

The narrative remains recognizably the same while almost everything it
is made to mean changes.

At first, I wanted to place the famous versions together.
[Caravaggio](https://judith.jameshoward.us/artworks/caravaggio-judith),
[Artemisia
Gentileschi](https://judith.jameshoward.us/artworks/artemisia-florence),
[Botticelli](https://judith.jameshoward.us/artworks/botticelli-return-bethulia),
[Donatello](https://judith.jameshoward.us/artworks/donatello-judith),
[Giorgione](https://judith.jameshoward.us/artworks/giorgione-judith),
[Klimt](https://judith.jameshoward.us/artworks/klimt-judith-i), and a
few others would have made a perfectly respectable little exhibition.

Respectability did not survive contact with the research.

An exhibition assembled from the dozen works everyone already knows
says something about the canon. It does not necessarily tell the
history of the subject. Institutional fame is not a proxy for artistic
importance, and search-engine visibility is not a curatorial principle.
Once the search moved outward from the object tradition rather than
downward through lists of famous museums, Judith began turning up
everywhere.

The resulting [gallery](https://judith.jameshoward.us/gallery) includes
paintings, sculpture, drawings, prints, manuscript illumination,
tapestry, photography, terracotta, ceremonial art, and decorative
objects. There is a [Safavid album painting made in
Isfahan](https://judith.jameshoward.us/artworks/muhammad-zaman-judith),
a [southern-Netherlandish
tapestry](https://judith.jameshoward.us/artworks/met-tapestry-judith),
a [carved powder
flask](https://judith.jameshoward.us/artworks/powder-flask-judith), a
[nineteenth-century terracotta
Judith](https://judith.jameshoward.us/artworks/french-terracotta-judith),
and a [Polish Hanukkah
lamp](https://judith.jameshoward.us/artworks/yeshiva-hanukkah-lamp-judith)
crowned by Judith holding the head of Holofernes. The lamp is the sort
of object that confirms the search has finally escaped the familiar
survey.

The famous works are still there. They are simply no longer permitted
to pretend they constitute the whole tradition.

Each object has its own catalogue record with information about
attribution, date, medium, dimensions, creation place, present location,
interpretation, and sources. Disputed dates and attributions are
identified as disputes. Surrogate sources are identified as surrogate
sources. The site does not silently convert the best available evidence
into certainty.

The exhibition also includes two complementary
[maps](https://judith.jameshoward.us/map). "Made here" shows where the
objects were created. "Held here" shows where they live now. The
distinction matters. A Brazilian artist may have painted a work in
Florence, while an Italian painting may now hang in Baltimore, Tehran,
Melbourne, or São Paulo. The maps can be zoomed, panned, and used to
open the works associated with each location.

This reveals two different geographies. One is the geography of
artistic production. The other is the geography produced by collecting,
trade, inheritance, conquest, donation, and the modern museum.

The [story itself](https://judith.jameshoward.us/story) is part of the
exhibition. The site includes [two complete English translations of the
Book of Judith](https://judith.jameshoward.us/read), chapter crosswalks,
and interpretive material connecting the text to the objects. This helps
separate elements actually found in the book from later artistic and
religious traditions. Judith's unnamed maid, for instance, is often
called Abra, although the name comes from later tradition rather than
the biblical text. The familiar association with [Hanukkah and
cheese](https://www.thetorah.com/article/judith-a-chanukah-heroine) also
has its own complicated history.

Of the 58 objects presently included, 43 have reusable images. The
remaining 15 are deliberately presented as text-only records because
suitable images are not available for reuse. Each still includes the
fullest defensible record and a link to the institution, holder, or
rightsholder possessing the image. The complete [image-rights
audit](https://judith.jameshoward.us/rights) explains the status and
source of every displayed or withheld image.

Omitting those works would make image licensing determine art history.
That seemed unacceptable.

Instead, the exhibition explains why the image is absent and includes a
tactfully pointed invitation for the holder or rightsholder to make one
available. This is museum-grade shade, but it is also a serious point.
Public collections cannot simultaneously lament their limited reach and
prevent the public from using images of the objects they preserve.

The exhibition does not claim completeness. Its [open research
ledger](https://judith.jameshoward.us/research) identifies what remains
missing, what could not yet be verified, and which areas of the
tradition remain underrepresented. The [revision
history](https://judith.jameshoward.us/about#changelog) records factual
and structural corrections rather than quietly pretending the first
version was flawless.

My work in history and genealogy has repeatedly taught me that absence
from the familiar story is not the same thing as absence from history.
A life reduced to birth, marriage, and death often becomes astonishing
once someone bothers to look for what happened between those dates. The
same principle applies here. Fame-weighted research produces thin
histories. Object-first research recovers things the familiar narrative
discarded.

This exhibition was built with extensive assistance from artificial
intelligence. [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com) assisted with collection
research, source organization, writing, site engineering, data
validation, and deployment. [Claude](https://claude.ai) was then
invited, repeatedly and with considerable enthusiasm, to attack the
result. It found factual errors, dead links, contradictory records, weak
explanations, and several things it merely disliked. The valid
criticisms were researched and corrected. A few findings were rejected
when the underlying collection records supported the exhibition.

That process is [disclosed as part of the
exhibition](https://judith.jameshoward.us/about) because it is part of
the project. Artificial intelligence made an exhibition of this breadth
practical for one person to construct. It did not make skepticism,
judgment, verification, or responsibility unnecessary.

The complete site is deployed from its [GitHub
repository](https://github.com/k3jph/judith.jameshoward.us), with every
change automatically tested and published through [GitHub
Actions](https://github.com/k3jph/judith.jameshoward.us/actions/workflows/deploy-pages.yml).
The exhibition now has a permanent home, a reproducible build, and a
structure that can continue growing as additional objects and better
evidence emerge.

A physical exhibition of this scope would be nearly impossible. The
objects are scattered among national museums, municipal collections,
churches, university museums, libraries, specialist institutions,
private collections, and collections on several continents. Some could
travel. Many could not. Some are famous. Others have nearly disappeared
from the familiar account because their holders are less prominent,
their catalogues are difficult to search, or their images are
unavailable.

A virtual exhibition has different rules.

It can place Rome beside Baltimore, Florence beside Tehran, and a
celebrated oil painting beside a manuscript leaf, tapestry, photograph,
terracotta sculpture, or Hanukkah lamp. It can [connect an image to the
passage that inspired it](https://judith.jameshoward.us/read). It can
[place works created centuries apart immediately beside one
another](https://judith.jameshoward.us/compare). It can preserve
[unresolved questions](https://judith.jameshoward.us/research) rather
than concealing them behind a wall label.

I wanted to see what happened if we put as many Judiths as we
responsibly could into the same room.

[This is that room.](https://judith.jameshoward.us)

And, yes, it absolutely slays.
